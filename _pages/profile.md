  <h2>A life in books, science, and questions about how medicines work in the real world</h2>
</section>

<section class="section-block">
  <div class="editorial-story">
    <p>I was born in Beirut, Lebanon, and grew up in a family of booksellers, so books were part of my life from the very beginning. As a child, I was the kind of person who set slightly unreasonable reading goals for himself. I loved science early on, but I also wanted to do something practical, something that could directly help people. That combination is what first drew me to pharmacy.</p>

    <p>During pharmacy school, I became increasingly interested in how we know whether medications are truly safe and effective outside the controlled environment of clinical trials. Learning about the thalidomide tragedy made that question feel morally urgent, not just intellectually interesting. It pushed me toward drug safety, evidence generation, and the problem of what happens when our methods fall short of the decisions people have to make in real life.</p>

    <p>That curiosity led me to a master's degree in pharmacoepidemiology, where I studied older adults' knowledge of stroke signs and symptoms and saw how evidence can expose gaps that matter for public health. I later completed a PhD in Health Services and Outcomes Research at Purdue University, working in the Campbell Lab on questions related to medication use, aging, and brain health. My dissertation focused on how cumulative exposure to anticholinergic medications may increase the risk of delirium in hospitalized older adults.</p>

    <p>I am now a postdoctoral researcher at Purdue, working with Dr. John Allen on oncology pharmacoepidemiology and health disparities research. My current work uses large real-world datasets, including SEER-Medicare, together with causal inference methods such as target trial emulation, to study supportive care medication use and outcomes in older adults with cancer. Across all of my research, one theme has stayed consistent: I care about generating evidence that is rigorous enough to stand up scientifically, but also useful enough to matter in the real world.</p>

    <p>During my training, I also completed internships in HEOR and Global Evidence and Outcomes research, which strengthened my interest in industry and in the broader challenge of turning complex data into decisions that can improve patients' lives. More generally, I enjoy making difficult ideas easier to understand, whether in teaching, presentations, or collaborative research, and that has become one of the strengths I value most in my work.</p>
  </div>
</section>
